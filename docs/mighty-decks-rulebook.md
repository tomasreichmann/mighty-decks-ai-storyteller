# Mighty Decks

*A Fast-Paced Narrative Tabletop RPG*

> **Draft rules text.** This document consolidates the current Mighty Decks rules and the clarifications established during development. Illustration briefs are included inline for layout and art direction.

---

## 1. What Is Mighty Decks?

**Mighty Decks** is a fast-paced tabletop roleplaying game for **1–4 players and one Storyteller**.

Players describe what their characters do. When an action is risky, uncertain, or dramatically important, they resolve it by choosing a card from their personal **Outcome Deck** instead of rolling dice.

Because players choose from cards in hand, they have some control over when they succeed and when they accept trouble. The deck still creates uncertainty because every played card is replaced by a new draw.

Mighty Decks focuses on:

- dramatic storytelling,
- player agency,
- tactical tension,
- fast resolution,
- low bookkeeping,
- consequences that move the story forward.

The rules are **setting-independent**. The same mechanics can handle sword fights, starship repairs, courtroom arguments, monster hunts, heists, chases, survival stories, or almost anything else.

> **Illustration — The whole game at a glance**  
> A compact tabletop scene with three players and a Storyteller. Show Outcome cards in player hands, Stunts and Assets in front of players, Actor cards facing the players, Location cards forming a small scene, an Effect card beside a player, and a Counter card with a die on it. The goal is to communicate that the game is played through physical cards arranged around a shared scene rather than through character sheets or a grid.

---

## 2. What You Need to Play

- **1 Storyteller** — describes the world, presents challenges, controls NPCs, and interprets consequences.
- **1–4 Players** — each plays one main character.
- **An Adventure** — a prepared scenario or an original story.
- **Outcome Decks** — one 12-card deck for each player.
- **Stunt Cards** — talents, abilities, specialties, and signature moves.
- **Effect Cards** — conditions and consequences such as Injury, Distress, Stuck, or Boost.
- **Asset Cards** — equipment, tools, weapons, vehicles, consumables, and other useful things.
- **Actor Cards** — characters and creatures controlled by the Storyteller, and optionally illustrations for player characters.
- **Counter Cards** — progress, threats, countdowns, resources, and other changing scene values.
- **Location Cards or a zone map** — used when positioning matters.
- **Dice used as markers** — typically d4, d6, d8, and d12. Dice are not rolled to resolve actions; they show current values on Counters and Actors.

All components needed by a published Adventure should be included or clearly listed.

---

## 3. The Outcome Deck

Each player normally has an identical **12-card Outcome Deck**:

- 1 × **Special Action**
- 3 × **Success**
- 3 × **Partial Success**
- 4 × **Fumble**
- 1 × **Chaos**

At the start of play, shuffle your deck and draw **3 cards**.

If your entire starting hand consists only of Fumbles, redraw it.

Some Stunts or special rules can change hand size. When a rule changes your hand size, use that new size whenever you refill your hand unless the rule says otherwise.

When you need to draw and your Outcome Deck is empty, shuffle the discard pile to form a new deck and continue drawing.

## 4. The Core Action Loop

Use Outcome cards only when the result matters.

If an action is obvious, safe, or has no meaningful consequence for failure, simply let it happen and continue the story.

When an action is risky or dramatically important:

1. **Describe your intent.** Say what your character is trying to accomplish.
2. **Establish the stakes.** The Storyteller explains the important risk or consequence if things go badly. Exact Effect cards do not always need to be announced in advance.
3. **Play an Outcome card.** Choose a legal card from your hand.
4. **Apply modifiers.** Add or subtract relevant Stunts, Assets, Effects, and other modifiers.
5. **Resolve the Effect.** The Storyteller interprets what the total Effect means in the fiction.
6. **Discard and redraw.** Discard the played Outcome card and immediately draw a replacement.
7. **Check for Catastrophe.** If your new hand triggers a Catastrophe, announce and resolve it immediately.

### Example — A Basic Action

Mira wants to climb a crumbling wall before guards arrive.

**Storyteller:** “If this goes badly, you could fall or get stuck halfway.”

Mira plays **Success: +2 Effect**.

**Storyteller:** “You reach the top quickly and find a good vantage point.”

Mira discards the Success and immediately draws a replacement Outcome card.

## 5. Outcome Cards

### 5.1 Success

**Success: +2 Effect**

The action works well. Apply **+2 Effect**, before other modifiers.

If the action only needed part of that Effect, excess Effect may improve the result when it makes sense: the action may be faster, quieter, more complete, or create an additional advantage.

A Success does not need its own complication unless the situation or another rule creates one.

---

### 5.2 Partial Success

**Partial Success: +1 Effect**

A Partial Success usually means the action works, but with reduced impact, a complication, or another imperfect result. The Storyteller chooses what fits the action and the current stakes.

A Partial Success does **not** always create a Complication card.

### Example — Partial Success

Brother Tomas tells a suspicious guard that the party was summoned by the lord of the keep.

He plays **Partial Success: +1 Effect**.

The Storyteller decides that Tomas gets what he wanted, but not cleanly.

**Storyteller:** “The guard lets you through, but follows you to verify your story.”

---

### 5.3 Fumble

**Fumble: roughly −1 Effect**

A Fumble means something goes wrong. The exact result depends on the action and the fiction.

A useful guideline is that one Fumble creates roughly **one significant negative Effect**. This might be:

- 1 Injury,
- 1 Distress,
- a Complication,
- Stuck or Hindered,
- a negative change to a Counter,
- a Boost for an enemy,
- lost time, ammunition, position, or another meaningful setback.

A Fumble does **not** always mean that the attempted action simply fails. The Storyteller may allow some useful part of the action to happen and attach a consequence instead.

### Example — Two Valid Fumbles

Mira fires her bow at a bandit and plays a **Fumble**.

**Simple failure:**

**Storyteller:** “The arrow flies wide and strikes the wall.”

The attack does no useful Effect.

**Success with a larger consequence:**

**Storyteller:** “The arrow hits for the bow's normal 1 Injury, but the bowstring snaps.”

The bow cannot be fired again until Mira repairs it. The Storyteller may represent this with a **Complication** attached to the bow Asset.

Both are valid Fumble results. The Storyteller chooses the one that best fits the scene.

---

### 5.4 Chaos

**Chaos: something unexpected happens.**

Chaos is neither a normal success nor a normal failure. The Storyteller changes the situation in an unpredictable way that is **roughly neutral overall**: it may help in one way while creating a new problem in another.

Chaos cannot be used for Defense.

### Example — Chaos

Sir Aldren tries to smash through a locked door and plays **Chaos**.

**Storyteller:** “Before your hammer lands, the door opens from the other side. A surprised guard is standing there.”

A different Chaos result might be:

> Mira misses an enemy with her shot but hits a fire extinguisher, filling the room with obscuring mist.

Chaos should change the situation rather than behave like a disguised Success or Fumble.

---

### 5.5 Special Action

**Special Action: +3 Effect**

A Special Action is the strongest normal Outcome card, but it is not legal for every action.

You may play it when one of the following applies:

- a relevant Stunt explicitly allows or requires a Special Action,
- the action clearly falls within an established area of expertise for the character,
- another rule explicitly allows it,
- the Storyteller agrees that the character's background makes the action exceptional.

Having any Stunt does **not** automatically make Special Action legal. The Stunt must explicitly say so, or the character must otherwise qualify.

Areas of expertise are deliberately broad and narrative. A former city guard might reasonably count firearms or weapons training, guard procedure, questioning suspects, and perhaps driving or riding under pressure as expertise, but not hacking, advanced medicine, or siege engineering without further justification.

When in doubt, favor a clear character concept over technical loopholes.

## 6. Effect: How Much Changes

**Effect measures the amount of impact an action has. It is not the same thing as damage.**

The Outcome card gives the action a base Effect:

- Special Action: **+3**
- Success: **+2**
- Partial Success: **+1**
- Fumble: usually about **one negative Effect**
- Chaos: unpredictable, not measured normally

Then add or subtract relevant modifiers from Assets, Stunts, Effects, the environment, or special rules.

Unless a rule says otherwise, modifiers **stack**.

The Storyteller interprets the final total according to what the character actually did.

A successful sword strike may turn +2 Effect into **2 Injury**. Convincing a frightened enemy to surrender may create **Distress**. Repairing a machine may add progress to a **Counter**. Pulling someone free may remove **Stuck**. A clever defensive maneuver may create a **Boost**.

### Example — Effect Is Impact, Not Damage

A heavy chandelier hangs above a guard.

Instead of shooting the guard directly, Mira shoots the chain holding it and plays **Success: +2 Effect**.

The Storyteller agrees that the fiction supports the attempt and resolves the +2 Effect as:

- **1 Distress**
- **1 Stuck**

**Storyteller:** “The chandelier crashes beside him. He dives away in panic but is trapped beneath fallen furniture.”

The Outcome determined **how much** happened. The fictional action determined **what** happened.

> **Layout callout:** **Outcome determines the amount of Effect. Fiction determines what that Effect means.**

## 7. Effects

Effect cards represent ongoing conditions, harm, advantages, and problems. Place them where everyone can see them unless secrecy matters.

Effects remain until their removal condition is met, the Storyteller rules that they no longer make sense, or another rule removes them.

### 7.1 Injury

Injury represents physical harm.

A character can normally hold up to **4 Injury**. Some Stunts or special rules may change this limit.

At maximum Injury, the character is **Taken Out**.

See **Taken Out and Recovery** below.

---

### 7.2 Distress

Distress represents fear, exhaustion, emotional strain, confusion, shock, or other mental pressure.

At **3 Distress**, the character becomes **Panicked**.

While Panicked, the player cannot freely choose an Outcome card. When they must play one, they use the top card of their Outcome Deck instead.

At **4 Distress**, the character becomes **Hopeless** instead.

While Hopeless, the character can only flee, give up, or otherwise withdraw from the source of the crisis until their Distress is reduced.

If Distress falls from 4 to 3, Hopeless ends and Panicked applies again. Below 3 Distress, Panicked ends.

### Distress Thresholds

Mira reaches 3 Distress and becomes Panicked. Later she gains a fourth Distress and becomes Hopeless. If Tomas helps her recover one Distress, she returns to 3 and is Panicked again.

---

### 7.3 Boost

A Boost is a temporary advantage.

Unless a card says otherwise, a Boost can provide:

- **+1 Effect** to the next relevant Action or Defense, or
- **+1 Move** when appropriate.

Discard it after use.

Boosts may belong to a player, Actor, Asset, Location, or situation when that makes sense.

---

### 7.4 Complication

A Complication represents a temporary problem that makes something harder.

Unless the Storyteller explains a different effect, a Complication gives **−1 Effect the next time it applies, then is discarded**.

A Complication can be attached to whatever it affects:

- a character — twisted ankle,
- an Asset — jammed gun or damaged bow,
- a Location — unstable rubble,
- an Actor — distracted or exposed,
- a situation — suspicious guards.

The Storyteller explains what the Complication means and how it can be removed.

Some Complications behave differently. For example, a snapped bowstring might make the bow unusable until a character spends an Action repairing it instead of simply applying −1 Effect once.

---

### 7.5 Hindered

A Hindered character cannot normally **Move and take an Action in the same turn**. They must choose one.

Hindered ends when the character frees themselves or the fictional cause no longer applies.

---

### 7.6 Stuck

A Stuck character cannot Move.

They may still act if the fiction allows it. Freeing themselves, receiving help, or removing the cause discards Stuck.

Multiple Stuck cards are rarely useful unless a specific Adventure or rule gives them a special meaning.

---

### 7.7 Burning

Burning represents dangerous heat or fire.

At the end of a turn in which a character has **2 or more Burning**, they gain **1 Injury** unless the relevant rule says otherwise.

---

### 7.8 Freezing

Freezing represents dangerous cold.

At the end of a turn in which a character has **2 or more Freezing**, they gain **1 Distress** unless the relevant rule says otherwise.

---

## 8. Catastrophe

A Catastrophe is triggered when, after drawing, your hand contains:

- **3 or more Fumbles**, or
- **only Fumbles**, even if a Stunt or special rule has reduced your hand size below 3.

The Catastrophe is checked **after the action or Defense that caused the draw has fully resolved**.

### Catastrophe Sequence

1. Resolve the current Action or Defense normally.
2. Discard the played Outcome card.
3. Draw its replacement.
4. If your new hand triggers a Catastrophe, announce it immediately.
5. The Storyteller resolves the Catastrophe approximately as if the triggering Fumbles had been played together.
6. Discard the Fumbles that triggered it.
7. Draw replacements up to your current hand size.
8. If the new hand somehow triggers another Catastrophe, resolve it again.

For a normal three-card hand, a Catastrophe usually represents roughly **three negative Effects**.

Whenever possible, spread those consequences across the fiction instead of simply dealing 3 Injury. A Catastrophe should create a dramatic crisis.

Possible consequences include:

- Injury or Distress,
- Complications,
- Stuck or Hindered,
- damaged Assets,
- enemy Boosts,
- lost progress,
- advancing danger Counters,
- losing position or equipment,
- attracting a new threat.

A Catastrophe **can** Take Out a character when that makes sense, especially if they were already near their limit, but the recommended default is to bring characters to the brink rather than remove them from play immediately.

### Example — Catastrophe

Mira takes her Action normally and then draws a replacement Outcome card.

Her hand becomes:

**Fumble / Fumble / Fumble**

She immediately announces a **Catastrophe**.

The Storyteller resolves it as three connected consequences:

- Mira gains **1 Injury**.
- Her bow gains a **Complication**.
- A nearby bandit gains a **Boost**.

**Storyteller:** “Loose stones give way beneath you. You slam into the wall, damage your bow, and leave yourself open to the bandit.”

Mira discards the three Fumbles and redraws three cards.

### Unusual Hand Sizes

If a character has a four-card hand and holds four Fumbles, a Catastrophe triggers normally. Discard **three Fumbles** for the Catastrophe and redraw three; the fourth remains in hand unless another rule changes this.

If a character has a two-card hand and both cards are Fumbles, the whole hand is Fumbles, so a Catastrophe triggers. Discard both and redraw two, assuming the reduced hand size still applies.

From the three Fumbles, branch to three different consequences: an Injury on the character, a Complication on a bow Asset, and a Boost on an enemy Actor. Include a clear arrow showing that the original action resolves before the Catastrophe begins.

## 9. Characters, Expertise, Stunts, and Assets

Mighty Decks does not require a traditional character sheet. A character can begin with a strong concept, a few Stunts, and whatever Assets the Adventure provides.

### 9.1 Character Concept and Expertise

A character's background establishes what they are plausibly good at.

Use common sense. If the character concept clearly supports expertise in an action, the Storyteller may allow **Special Action**.

Be generous when the connection is natural, but reject tenuous attempts to turn one broad background into expertise at everything.

---

### 9.2 Stunts

Stunts represent talents, unusual abilities, learned techniques, supernatural powers, background advantages, or signature moves.

Players normally choose **2 Stunts** during setup unless the Adventure says otherwise.

A Stunt may:

- add or subtract Effect,
- change range,
- allow additional targets,
- change movement,
- alter hand size,
- allow multiple Outcome cards,
- enable or require Special Action,
- create exceptions to normal rules.

The text on a Stunt overrides the general rules when they conflict.

---

### 9.3 Assets

Assets represent useful things in the fiction: weapons, armor, tools, vehicles, keys, information, magical items, medical supplies, and more.

Assets can:

- add Effect,
- reduce incoming Effect,
- establish what an action can do,
- define range or damage type,
- allow actions that would otherwise be impossible,
- carry Complications or Boosts,
- be consumed.

Assets may be built from a **base card plus one or more modifier cards in a sleeve**, allowing the physical object to change during play.

### Example — Stacking Modifiers

Mira uses a magical throwing knife that gives **+1 Effect when thrown**.

She also has a Stunt giving **+1 Effect with thrown weapons**.

She attacks and plays **Success: +2 Effect**.

Total Effect:

**2 Outcome + 1 Asset + 1 Stunt = 4 Effect**

Unless a rule says otherwise, applicable modifiers stack.

> **Illustration — Composed Asset**  
> Show a base Asset card, such as a Throwing Knife, inside a transparent sleeve with a visible modifier card such as Returning Blade. Beside it, show a relevant Stunt and a Success Outcome card flowing into a simple equation: `2 Outcome + 1 Asset + 1 Stunt = 4 Effect`. The illustration should emphasize physical card composition, not fantasy spectacle.

---

### 9.4 Consumables

A Consumable is an Asset that is discarded after use.

A player can normally use **one Consumable per round**.

A Consumable may be used on its own if its text gives an automatic Effect, or combined with an Outcome card as part of an Action.

If the player uses an Outcome card for the same task as the Consumable, the Outcome and Consumable Effects combine. That Outcome card is the player's Action for the round.

A Consumable can also be used during Defense if the item's text and the fiction support it.

### Example — Healing with a Consumable

Aldren has 3 Injury.

Tomas uses a Bandage that provides **+1 recovery Effect** and plays **Partial Success: +1 Effect** as part of the same healing Action.

Total recovery Effect is **2**, so Aldren removes **2 Injury**.

Because Tomas used an Outcome card for the healing, that was his Action for the round.

Alternatively, Tomas could use only the Consumable for its listed Effect and still use his Outcome card for a different Action, if the Consumable does not itself require the Action.

---

## 10. Actors and Toughness

Actor cards represent NPCs, creatures, hazards, or other characters that matter in a scene.

Player characters may also use Actor cards for illustration, but the Storyteller usually controls Actors.

### 10.1 Toughness

Most Actors do not need separate Injury and Distress cards. Instead, they have **Toughness**: the amount of Injury and Distress they can sustain before being Taken Out.

Only **Injury and Distress** reduce Toughness by default.

Stuck, Hindered, Burning, Freezing, Complications, and other Effects matter normally but do not reduce Toughness unless a special rule says otherwise.

Track **remaining Toughness** with a die placed directly on the Actor card.

If an Actor has Toughness 3, place a die showing **3** on the Actor. If it suffers 2 Injury, turn the die to **1**. At **0**, the Actor is Taken Out.

The Storyteller may use actual Injury and Distress cards on an Actor when the distinction matters, but dice are usually faster and preserve Effect cards for players.

### 10.2 Taken Out Actors

Taken Out does not necessarily mean dead.

Depending on what happened, an Actor may:

- surrender,
- flee,
- fall unconscious,
- become trapped,
- stop fighting to tend wounds,
- lose the will to continue,
- be killed, if the fiction clearly supports it.

The Storyteller narrates the result.

### Example — Injury and Distress Combine

A Bandit has **Toughness 3**.

Sir Aldren attacks with a sword and plays **Success: +2 Effect**. The bandit suffers **2 Injury**, so the die on the Actor card changes from **3 to 1**.

Later Brother Tomas intimidates the wounded bandit and gets **Partial Success: +1 Distress**.

The bandit's remaining Toughness reaches **0**.

**Storyteller:** “Bleeding and surrounded, he throws down his weapon.”

The bandit is **Taken Out** even though the final Effect was mental rather than physical.

> **Illustration — Remaining Toughness**  
> Show one Actor card in three small states: die showing 3, then 1 after taking 2 Injury, then 0/Taken Out after 1 Distress. Use icons or small labels to show that both Injury and Distress reduce the same remaining Toughness value.

## 11. Hidden Actors and Information

Actor cards are normally public once the characters can understand what they are facing. Toughness and listed abilities are normally visible.

The Storyteller may hide information when uncertainty is part of the scene.

A useful physical technique is to place an **Actor card face down** in front of a player. This can mean:

- something is present but unidentified,
- something is approaching,
- something has appeared but cannot yet be clearly perceived,
- the players know a threat will act but do not know its exact nature.

When the threat is revealed, turn the Actor card face up. Its Toughness and abilities then normally become public unless there is a specific reason to keep something hidden.

> **Illustration — Hidden Actor**  
> Show a face-down Actor card in front of a player with a small question-mark callout. Beside it show the same card flipped face up, now with visible illustration, abilities, and a Toughness die.

## 12. Scenes and Tension

An Adventure is divided into **Scenes**.

Scenes are either broadly **High Tension** or **Low Tension**, although the Storyteller does not need to label every moment formally.

### 12.1 High Tension

High Tension scenes include combat, chases, crises, moral confrontations, dangerous rituals, disasters, or anything where timing matters.

Turn-based play is often useful here.

### 12.2 Low Tension

Low Tension scenes include travel, investigation without immediate danger, downtime, conversations, recovery, planning, and character moments.

Play is usually freeform.

A Low Tension scene often gives characters a reasonable chance to recover. See **Recovery**.

### Pacing

Alternate tension when possible. Constant crisis becomes exhausting; constant safety removes pressure.

After a difficult High Tension scene, even a short Low Tension scene can create room for roleplay, consequences, planning, and recovery.

## 13. Turn-Based Play

Use turn-based play when timing and order matter.

Before the Adventure begins, the group should agree on a simple player order. **Clockwise or counter-clockwise around the table is recommended** because it keeps rounds fast and obvious.

### 13.1 Player Turn

A normal player turn allows:

- **1 Move**, and
- **1 Action**.

These can normally happen in either order.

The Storyteller may allow **two Moves instead of Move + Action** when it makes sense, but this is a suggested ruling rather than a universal entitlement.

A player's Action is usually resolved by one Outcome card when a risky resolution is needed.

### 13.2 Actor Turns

NPCs do **not** play Outcome cards.

Their attacks and abilities have fixed Effects. This keeps Storyteller bookkeeping low and leaves the important card choices with the players.

Place an Actor card in front of the player it acts after. The Actor usually attacks or interacts with that nearby player, but it is not forced to do so.

If several Actors are in front of the same player, they all act after that player. The Storyteller chooses their order.

An Actor can be moved in front of another player when the fiction changes. This changes both its obvious focus and when it will normally act in later rounds.

> **Illustration — Turn order around the table**

Draw arrows: Player 1 → Player 2 → Actor → Actor → Player 3 → Player 4 → Actor → back to Player 1. Add a small note that an Actor card can move in front of another player when the situation changes.

## 14. Locations, Zones, Movement, and Range

When exact positioning does not matter, use normal narrative description.

When it matters, divide the scene into **Zones**.

A Zone can be represented by:

- a Location card,
- a room,
- an area marked on a map,
- a section of terrain,
- an abstract range such as Close / Near / Far.

Zones do not need to be the same size. They represent meaningful areas of action, not exact measurements.

### 14.1 Movement

One Move normally lets a character move to an **adjacent Zone**.

Stunts, Assets, vehicles, terrain, or Effects may modify this.

### 14.2 Range

The Storyteller decides what ranges make sense unless a card gives a specific rule.

Useful defaults:

- a punch or sword strike — same Zone,
- a thrown object — adjacent Zone,
- an ordinary ranged weapon — often up to 2 Zones,
- a sniper weapon — anywhere in sight,
- artillery — anywhere the shot can reasonably reach ballistically.

If a target is outside the action's range, the action is normally **not possible** rather than merely taking a numerical penalty.

Stunts and Assets can change these permissions. A Stunt might let a character throw one Zone farther. A magical throwing knife might give +1 Effect and return to the wielder after the attack.

### Example — Range and Zones

The scene uses three Location cards:

**Gate → Courtyard → Tower**

Mira is at the Gate. A bandit is in the Tower.

Mira's bow can attack up to two Zones away, so she can shoot the bandit.

Aldren's sword only attacks targets in the same Zone, so he must move closer before he can strike.

> **Illustration — Zones and range**  
> Show three connected Location cards: Gate, Courtyard, Tower. Place Mira at Gate and a Bandit at Tower. Overlay range arrows: sword = same Zone, thrown weapon = 1 Zone, bow = 2 Zones, long-range weapon = line of sight. Add a tiny inset showing irregular Zones on a map, such as a large courtyard connected to a narrow stairway and a small balcony, to reinforce that Zones are narrative rather than equal-sized spaces.

## 15. Attacks

Attacks work like any other risky Action.

The player describes what they are doing, chooses an Outcome card, applies modifiers, and the Storyteller converts the resulting Effect into consequences.

A normal physical attack often causes Injury, but it does not have to.

Weapons and Stunts can:

- add Effect,
- change range,
- change damage type,
- ignore armor,
- inflict multiple Effect types,
- attack multiple targets,
- create special consequences.

### 15.1 One Target by Default

An Action normally affects **one logical target**.

Attacking multiple targets requires the fiction, an Asset, a Stunt, Splash, or another specific rule to allow it.

### 15.2 Distributed Effect

Effect is not normally distributed among multiple targets unless a Stunt or other rule explicitly allows it.

A Stunt may, for example, let a player divide 3 Effect as 2 Injury to one target and 1 Injury to another.

---

## 16. Defense

When a player character is attacked or threatened by an Effect that can logically be resisted, they may play an Outcome card to defend.

**Chaos cannot be used for Defense.**

During a round, a player may play one Outcome card for their Action on their own turn and any number of legal Outcome cards as Defenses when threatened.

Defense Effect reduces the incoming Effect.

If the player chooses not to defend, resolve the full incoming Effect as if their Defense provided no reduction.

A defensive Fumble normally gives **no protection**, but it does not automatically increase the incoming Injury or other Effect. The Storyteller may add another fitting consequence, such as a Complication, but should generally avoid turning a 2-Injury attack into 3 Injury merely because the Defense was a Fumble.

### Example — Defense

A Bandit attacks Aldren for **2 Injury**.

| Defense | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Success (+2) | 0 Injury |
| Partial Success (+1) | 1 Injury |
| Fumble | 2 Injury |
| No Defense | 2 Injury |

Aldren plays Success.

Because his Defense exceeds what was needed, the Storyteller may let the excess create a small advantage.

**Storyteller:** “You catch his axe on your shield and force him off balance. Take a Boost.”

Excess Defense can create a Boost or narrative advantage, but does not normally become a free counterattack.

If Aldren wants to punch first, that is an Action, not a Defense; he needs an explicit Stunt that allows a counterattack to make that punch as part of his Defense.

### 16.1 Different Types of Incoming Effect

Defense can reduce Injury, Distress, Burning, Stuck, or other incoming Effects when the fictional defense makes sense.

A shield might help against arrows but not fear. Closing your eyes might help against a hypnotic gaze but not a falling boulder. Use the fiction first.

---

## 17. Splash and Area Effects

**Splash** applies the listed Effect to **every valid target in the affected Zone**.

Do not divide Splash Effect between targets. Each target receives the full listed Effect.

Each player defends separately.

### Tiny Example — Splash

A dragon breathes fire into the Courtyard for **2 Burning, Splash**.

Mira and Aldren are both in the Courtyard. Each receives the full 2 Burning and may defend separately.

Characters in adjacent Zones are unaffected unless the attack says otherwise.

---

## 18. Recovery

Recovery follows the same Effect logic as other actions.

Unless the Storyteller says otherwise, an Action can remove as many relevant Effect cards as its total positive Effect.

A healer who gets **Success: +2 Effect** on an appropriate treatment can normally remove **2 Injury**. A Stunt adding +1 Effect could raise that to 3.

Recovery can come from:

- an Outcome-card Action,
- a Consumable,
- an Asset,
- a Stunt,
- rest or appropriate behavior in a Low Tension scene,
- another Adventure-specific rule.

You can sometimes remove Distress by catching your breath, resting, talking, indulging a vice, praying, or another appropriate action, if the Storyteller agrees.

### 18.1 Low Tension Recovery

Low Tension scenes normally allow characters to recover **all Injury and Distress when they have a reasonable opportunity to rest and recover**.

The Storyteller may also discard other Effects that no longer make narrative sense.

Adventures focused on survival, attrition, scarcity, horror, or resource management may explicitly override this and use stricter recovery rules.

When recovery is not automatic, it is usually better to resolve it by cycling Outcome cards or spending Consumables rather than granting free healing during a High Tension scene.

---

## 19. Taken Out

When a player character reaches their maximum Injury, they are **Taken Out**.

Taken Out is intentionally broad. It means the character can no longer contribute meaningfully toward the current High Tension scene goal until they recover.

Depending on the fiction, Taken Out might mean:

- unconscious,
- badly wounded,
- pinned or trapped,
- overwhelmed,
- unable to fight,
- forced to focus entirely on survival.

Taken Out does **not** automatically mean dying.

Another character can help them recover through an Action, Consumable, or other appropriate method. If the High Tension scene ends and the situation becomes safe enough for a Low Tension scene, the Taken Out character may recover normally if the fiction allows it.

### Example — Taken Out

Aldren reaches his maximum of 4 Injury during a battle.

He is Taken Out and can no longer help defeat the bandits.

Tomas could use his turn to treat Aldren. If the bandits are defeated first and the next scene becomes Low Tension, Aldren may instead recover once the group has a reasonable chance to rest.

---

## 20. Counters

Counters track anything that changes over time and matters to the scene.

Examples include:

- progress toward a goal,
- danger,
- time,
- escape,
- investigation,
- ritual completion,
- morale,
- structural collapse,
- weather,
- resources.

Place a die on the Counter card showing its current value.

The Storyteller should explain what the Counter represents and what important values mean.

Counters can move **up or down** when the fiction supports it. They normally do not go below 0 unless the Adventure explicitly allows it.

A Counter can have:

- a final target,
- intermediate triggers,
- ongoing effects based on its current value,
- several meaningful thresholds.

The wording and narration should make clear whether an event happens once or remains active.

If Security arrives when a Counter reaches 3, reducing the Counter later does not make the guards disappear, and reaching 3 again does not summon a second identical security team unless the Adventure says so.

### Example — Progress Counter

The Storyteller announces:

**Raise the Portcullis — 0/4**

Aldren forces the mechanism and plays Success.

The Counter rises from **0 to 2**.

Later Mira Fumbles while helping. The Storyteller decides the mechanism slips and reduces the Counter from **2 to 1**.

At **4**, the portcullis opens.

### Example — Ongoing Counter

The Storyteller places:

**Ice Storm — 3/4**

The Adventure says:

> At the end of each round, exposed characters gain 1 Distress for each current level of Ice Storm.

At 3/4, exposed characters therefore gain **3 Distress** at the end of the round.

At 4/4, the Adventure may specify an additional event, such as the storm becoming impossible to endure outside shelter.

> **Illustration — Counter vs. Toughness dice**  
> Show two cards side by side. On the left, an Ice Storm Counter with a d4 showing 3 and caption `Counter value: 3/4`. On the right, a Bandit Actor with a die showing 1 and caption `Remaining Toughness: 1`. Emphasize that the same physical dice are markers whose meaning comes from the card they sit on.

---

## 21. Combining Different Kinds of Effect

The Storyteller may split positive Effect into different results when the action clearly supports it.

For example, a successful action with +2 Effect might:

- advance an Escape Counter by 1 and grant a Boost,
- cause 1 Distress and 1 Stuck,
- remove 1 Injury and remove a related Complication.

Do not split Effect merely to optimize numbers. The result should follow naturally from what the character did.

---

## 22. Narrating Outcomes

The Storyteller usually narrates the final outcome because they control the world, NPC reactions, and consequences.

Players should still have room to contribute.

The Storyteller can invite a player to describe important moments:

- “Describe how you defeated him.”
- “What does your spell look like?”
- “How did you actually fail to defend against the punch?”

The Storyteller keeps final authority over consequences involving the world and NPCs, while players remain the primary authors of their own character's intent, attitude, and style.

---

## 23. Ending a Scene

A Scene ends when:

- its goal is achieved,
- the threat is resolved,
- the situation changes enough that a new scene begins,
- the characters leave the location or conflict behind.

The Storyteller narrates the transition.

Effects normally carry over unless recovery or the fiction removes them.

A change from High Tension to Low Tension may allow recovery as described above.

---

## 24. Ending a Session

A session can end after:

- a major turning point,
- an emotional resolution,
- the end of an important Scene,
- a cliffhanger.

To preserve the physical state of an ongoing Adventure, take a photograph of the table showing player cards, Effects, Assets, Counters, Actors, and other persistent elements.

---

## 25. Finishing an Adventure

When an Adventure ends, briefly reflect on what changed:

- What happened to each character?
- Which relationships changed?
- Which injuries or consequences lasted?
- What did the characters gain or lose?
- What unresolved problem might return later?

The group can continue with the same characters or begin a new Adventure.

---

## 26. Optional Lethal Rules

The normal Taken Out rules are intentionally forgiving. Adventures that want death to be a real threat can use the following optional rule.

### 26.1 Death Draws

While Taken Out by Injury, the character may be unable to take normal Actions.

At the start of each of their turns or rounds, as specified by the Adventure, shuffle their Outcome cards as needed and draw **one card blindly from the top of the Outcome Deck**.

Track the results:

- each **Fumble** counts toward death,
- each **Success or Special Action** counts toward recovery.

When the character accumulates **3 Fumbles**, they die permanently.

When they accumulate **3 Successes and/or Special Actions**, discard **1 Injury**, clear the death-draw tally, and immediately return to normal play if they are now below their Taken Out threshold.

Other cards can be ignored or handled according to the Adventure's tone.

### 26.2 Sacrifice

A Taken Out character using the lethal rules may instead choose to **Sacrifice**.

They perform one final Action **as if they had played Special Action: +3 Effect**.

After the Action resolves, the character dies immediately.

Sacrifice should be a meaningful final choice, not a routine damage optimization.

## 27. Storyteller Principles

### Call for Outcome Cards Only When There Are Stakes

Do not ask for a card simply because a character does something.

If failure would be boring or meaningless, let the action happen.

### Explain the Stakes Before the Card

Players should understand the broad risk before choosing their Outcome.

You do not need to announce exact Effect cards in advance.

> “If this goes badly, you're probably going over the edge.”

is usually enough.

### Interpret Effect Through the Fiction

Outcome cards determine magnitude, not a fixed menu of moves.

If a player proposes a creative action that makes sense, let the Effect represent the natural result.

### Use Fumbles to Move the Story

A Fumble should create trouble, not a dead end.

Good Fumbles introduce cost, danger, pressure, damage, lost position, or difficult choices.

### Spread Catastrophe Consequences

Three different problems are usually more interesting than three copies of Injury.

### Keep NPCs Simple

NPCs have fixed attacks and abilities. They do not need their own Outcome hands.

Let players make the important tactical choices through Actions and Defense.

### Keep Information Visible by Default

Visible Actor Toughness and visible Counters help players make meaningful card choices.

Hide information only when uncertainty itself is interesting.

### Let the Adventure Set Difficulty

Mighty Decks is intentionally flexible about recovery, scarcity, lethality, and how harsh consequences should be.

A heroic one-shot can clear Injury and Distress quickly. A survival-horror Adventure can explicitly restrict healing, make ammunition matter, and use the lethal rules.

Explain these expectations before play.

## 29. Quick Reference

### Normal Turn

- Move + Action, in either order.
- Storyteller may allow two Moves instead.
- One Consumable may normally be used per round.

### Risky Action

1. State intent.
2. Storyteller explains stakes.
3. Play Outcome.
4. Add modifiers.
5. Resolve Effect.
6. Discard and redraw.
7. Check Catastrophe.

### Outcome Cards

| Card | Default |
| --- | --- |
| Special Action | +3 Effect; only when qualified |
| Success | +2 Effect |
| Partial Success | +1 Effect or imperfect success |
| Fumble | roughly one negative Effect |
| Chaos | unpredictable, roughly neutral; not Defense |

### Toughness

- Only Injury and Distress reduce Toughness by default.
- Track remaining Toughness with a die on the Actor.
- 0 remaining Toughness = Taken Out.

### Player Injury

- Maximum normally 4.
- At maximum: Taken Out.

### Distress

- 3 = Panicked.
- 4 = Hopeless.

### Movement

- 1 Move = usually 1 adjacent Zone.

### Defense

- Play a legal Outcome when threatened.
- Defense Effect reduces incoming Effect.
- Fumble or no Defense normally means taking the full Effect.
- Chaos cannot defend.

### Catastrophe

- Trigger: 3+ Fumbles, or your whole hand is Fumbles.
- Current Action resolves first.
- Resolve roughly one negative Effect per triggering Fumble.
- Spread consequences when possible.
- Discard triggering Fumbles and refill the hand.

# Design Philosophy

Mighty Decks is built around:

- **Narrative consequence over binary randomness**
- **Player agency through hand management**
- **Escalation instead of stagnation**
- **Fiction-first interpretation of Effect**
- **Visible tactical information with low bookkeeping**
- **Simple NPCs and meaningful player choices**
- **Alternating tension for pacing**
- **Rules that can bend to the Adventure's genre**

The cards should create pressure and opportunity without replacing roleplay. The rules decide **how much** a risky action changes the situation; the table decides **what that change means**.
